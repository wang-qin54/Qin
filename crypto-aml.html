02 · The problem

One investigation crossed four tools that were never designed to connect

OKLink's compliance suite is two products. On-chain AML raises alerts and profiles addresses and entities; Chaintelligence, a separate site, maps how funds move. One investigation passes through four of their surfaces, but each was built around its own data, and nobody had designed the path between them. Analysts exported data and finished cases in XMind and spreadsheets, so usage stayed low, even internally. New regulation in Hong Kong made the suite a priority.

The four stages of an investigation, and of this case
  1. Stage 1 · Triage Alert page · KYT "Is this alert worth a case?" Handed over
  2. Stage 2 · Assess Address risk · KYA "How risky is this address, and why?" Shipped
  3. Stage 3 · Identify Entity profiles · KYE "Who is behind the address?" Shipped · 0→1
  4. Stage 4 · Trace and report Fund-flow graph · Chaintelligence "Where did the money go?" Shipped
The brief

Let analysts run an investigation inside the product, from the alert that starts it to the evidence at the end, instead of exporting to work around it.

03 · Research

Observing 5 compliance officers work real cases set three design principles

While the team debated what to build, I proposed watching first. I interviewed 5 compliance officers across APAC and the US, sat in while they worked real cases, and mapped each step, the tool they used and where it broke. Each finding below became a principle that all four stages follow.

Findings & Principles
About 15 minutes per alert

Low and medium alerts get about 15 minutes. Anything an analyst has to hunt for comes out of that.

1Follow the analyst's reading order
Every decision gets reviewed

Decisions go to reviewers in other regions and, in the end, to regulators. A score nobody can explain doesn't survive that.

2Every score shows its source
The real analysis happens in XMind

The real synthesis happened outside the product: colour groups and notes in XMind, comparisons in spreadsheets.

3Bring the outside work inside
And one surprise

"It lacks features like A, B and C." Shown where they were: "Oh, I didn't know those features are there. They're what we need."

So each stage made existing features findable before adding new ones.
More from the sessionsWho they are, four gaps, two constraints
Who
Compliance investigators

AML analysts working the alert queue, and compliance officers at exchanges, OTC desks and institutions. Often on a small laptop or tablet, for a full shift.

Trigger
A flagged transaction

A transfer crosses a risk rule. The analyst asks who else is involved, how much moved, and whether it repeats. Data refreshes every 40–60 minutes.

Goal
A documented pattern

Close the case, escalate it, or export the fund-flow map as evidence for management or the regulator.

Four gaps behind the pain points
1Analysts think in cases; the tool thought in addresses

Three entry points (address graph, transaction map, transaction path) forced a technical choice before the analyst had any context.

2The layout hid direction

Hundreds of nodes around a centre showed what was connected, not which way the money moved.

3Some accounts carry no identity data

Broker sub-accounts have no KYC attached, so the first question is sub-account or master. The old page answered that only by copying an ID into a second tab.

4A small risk share isn't automatically safe

Analysts check how often a transfer repeats: many small deposits and one large withdrawal is a known laundering pattern.

Two constraints beyond the graph
Constraint
Labels stay internal

A label can name a person, and the bodies that receive a report don't share intelligence. Labels show to the analyst and stay off anything shared outward.

Request
Bulk tracing

"Ten addresses, then their common counterparties one layer out, two layers, up to twenty. Right now I do it in a spreadsheet." This became multi-hop tracing in section 05.

05 · Stage 2 · Assess

Address risk: every score traces back to the original sanctions listing

Address risk · On-chain AML / KYA Shipped 2Visible source

The FATF-aligned score breaks down in layers: five risk dimensions built from 14+ configurable risk factors, then three evidence tables that go down to the original sanctions listing. An analyst can answer "how do you know?" without leaving the page.

Detected risks: five dimensions on a radar, the categories that fired beside it, and three tabs into the evidence.

Each blocklist row names the category, the body that listed it, a link to the original listing, and the root address. The analyst can open the OFAC record instead of trusting the badge.

Rules for dense evidence, and multi-hop tracingMulti-hop handed over
  • Pagination only above five rows. Up to twenty rows scroll; beyond that the list stops rather than implying it's complete.
  • The category chart caps at eight slices. Long amounts switch to abbreviated notation, so a dense table stays readable.
  • Stablecoins and native coins resolve differently, so the transaction table treats each as its own case.
  • Unbuilt depth stays visible. It shows as a disabled control with a "not currently supported" note, so analysts learn where the tool stops instead of guessing.
Multi-hop tracing, from the bulk-tracing request

Trace a target address outward layer by layer, up to 50 addresses, with first and last transaction times on hover. I designed and specified it from the same research; it was with engineering when I left.

Multi-hop analysis: the risk-path list, one asset's transaction map, and the detail panel for a related address.

06 · Stage 3 · Identify

Entity profiles: a new module that shows who is behind an address

Entity profiles · On-chain AML / KYE Shipped · 0→1 2Visible source

An address tells you what moved, not who you're dealing with. KYE answers that: search an exchange, a mixer or a service and see its regulatory standing, transaction profile and main counterparties. I designed it from search and entity detail through to a watchlist, shipped in four releases.

The entity page: regulatory detail, transactions, risk activity and address list, over volume, category exposure and top counterparties.

  1. MVP18 Sep 2023Search, list and entity detail
  2. V1.1.020 Nov 2023Detail, list and watchlist
  3. V1.1.130 Nov 2023Design QA and a regression pass
  4. V1.1.226 Dec 2023Responsive layouts with permission variants

Each release had its own UI spec and a design QA walkthrough of the build.

The hard part: what each field may claimFour questions settled with the data team

Most open questions weren't about layout. They were about what the data could honestly support, and each was settled with product and the data team before the field shipped.

How precise is "established"?

Sources give a year for some entities and a full date for others. The field had to show both without implying precision it didn't have.

Fiat accepted: a count or a list?

A count fits the table; a list is what a compliance officer needs. The answer changed both the column and the detail card.

Who counts as the regulator?

An exchange can be licensed by several bodies in several jurisdictions, so "regulated" had to be defined before it could be drawn.

What should search match?

Exact, fuzzy or alias matching decides whether an analyst finds an entity under its local trading name or misses it.

07 · Stage 4 · Trace and report

Fund-flow graph: laid out the way money moves, and written up without leaving it

Fund-flow graph · Chaintelligence Shipped · 1-month redesign 1Reading order 3Work inside

The graph is where a case is built and handed on. The old one was a star that hid which way money moved, and the final write-up happened in XMind after an export. The redesign follows the analyst's steps from opening a graph to writing it up; the three biggest changes are below. Each tag says whether a user pain point or a design insight drove the change. Tap a screen to enlarge it.

1
Layout: from a star to a flow
User pain point

The radial layout became a left-to-right flow that reads the way money moves. The scattered tool rail became three groups (save and export, add and filter, edit), and edit actions appear only for what's selected.

With several addresses selected

The graph offers "Show mutual counterparts", the comparison analysts had been doing by hand in a spreadsheet.

2
Graph list: one list of cases
Design insight

Analysts build a graph around a case, not a single address. Three entry types became one list of case graphs, and light mode became the default for analysis.

3
Synthesise: bring the XMind step inside
Design insight

Analysts grouped evidence by colour and named the groups in XMind. Both moved into the tool, a colour palette for nodes and a summary label for a group, so the exported graph is already the report.

Three more steps: create, expand, addThe full six-step redesign
4
Create graph: start in the middle
Design insight

The entry moved from a bar at the top to the centre of the empty canvas, styled like the address node it creates, and it takes several addresses at once.

5
Source added: counterparties by default
User pain point

The old graph dropped one node onto an empty canvas. Now it expands one layer of senders and receivers automatically, so the analyst starts with the immediate picture.

6
Add more addresses from where you are
Design insight

Investigators gather suspicious addresses offline. They can now add them from a counterparty's related-addresses panel, from empty canvas, or from the transaction panel, not only from the top bar.

08 · System

Four shared components make the four stages read as one product

One risk scale, an exchange-logo library, light and dark themes, and responsive layouts with permission variants are shared across all four stages. Before each release, I checked the build against the spec.

Design QA and scope callsHow each release was checked, and two trade-offs
1

Global convention: a tooltip follows the direction of its arrow and always stays inside the content area. Each finding is boxed on the build, numbered and written up against the spec.

Deferred
Export watermark

I specified a watermark for exported graphs; engineering moved it out of the release, and export shipped without it.

Left out
An always-empty column

When a release couldn't supply risk-profile data, we removed the column rather than ship one that stayed blank and taught people to ignore it.

09 · Outcome

Three stages shipped, two handed over as specs

The fund-flow graph redesign, address risk analysis and the entity module shipped. The alert-page reorder and multi-hop tracing went to engineering as finished specs.

Shipped
  • Fund-flow graph: case-based entry, flow layout, contextual actions, in-tool synthesis
  • Event tracking on the graph, which had no usage funnel before
  • Address risk analysis with traceable scores
  • Entity module: MVP and three releases in 2023
Handed over
  • Alert page reordered around the analyst's reading order
  • Multi-hop tracing, designed and specified

I set up the measurement plan with the graph redesign, but adoption figures came in after I left, so there are no usage numbers here.

What I'd take to the next regulated product
1
Watch the work: the biggest problems never arrived as requests

The 15-minute budget, the detour into XMind and the copy-paste into a second tab never arrived as feature requests.

2
Follow how analysts reason, not how data is stored

Ordering four tools as one investigation mattered more than polishing any single screen.

3
Give every score a visible source

Every score, flag and conclusion needs a source on screen, because someone will be asked to defend it to a regulator.
