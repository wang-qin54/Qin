Institutional Verification Redesign (KYB)

// 2 weeks · UX/UI design · Web

// Role: lead designer on the flow — with 1 PM, front-end & back-end

Reframed an “add a selector” request into a root-cause redesign of document submission — for the institutional segment that drives 30–70% of exchange trading volume.

01 · Problem

The why

The request was cosmetic. The real problem was comprehension.

KYB is how institutional clients verify their company to trade. Institutional clients drive 30–70% of exchange volume — every drop-off here is lost revenue in the highest-value segment.

A user research report flagged a low KYB conversion rate. A feature request landed on my desk to “fix” it.

The request I was handed
“Add a company-type selector so users only see the fields relevant to them.”
A reasonable-sounding feature request.
  • What I found — traced it back to the user research report myself.
  • Why the request missed — heaviest drop-off sat on documents nearly every company type submits; filtering by company type wouldn’t touch the real bottleneck.
  • Two failure modes — users submitted without reading requirements and got rejected; others stalled at document upload and gave up.
  • Bottom line — of everyone who submitted documents, only 41% reached approved KYB.

03 · Approach

Benchmark the pattern, then decide what to keep

Competitor research was an input — alternatives were my decision.

What leading platforms do

Three patterns recurred across Binance, OKX, Coinbase and Kraken — inputs to my solution, not the answer.

  • Pre-select entity type — route users by institution type upfront.
  • Document checklist — show what to prepare before they start.
  • Plain-language instructions — readable requirements at each step.

Benchmarking institutional verification flows across leading exchanges.

Alternatives I considered — and how each earned its place
Option A · kept for scale

Company-type selector

Today’s bottleneck — doesn’t fix comprehension failures.
At scale — as EU and AU regulation expands, document requirements diverge by region; the selector routes the right set per market.

Option B · supporting role

Help text

Users already skip long text, so I used it only for basic concept explanation — never as the fix for complex requirements.

Option C · core fix

Guided required choice

Routes users to the right document with the lowest cognitive load — no hard reading required. This carried the redesign.

04 · Solution

The build

Turn reading into a guided, unskippable decision.

  • 1. Guided selection — the board / no-board distinction became a required choice that routes users to the correct document — the step that was 67% wrong before.
  • 2. Visual hierarchy — requirements are scannable instead of buried — directly targeting the 24%-wrong shareholder step.
  • 3. Additional upload section — raises approval chances when compliance requests further documents after the first submission.

05 · Edge cases

The messy parts

The states a happy-path demo hides.

1 / 3

More materials required

High-risk applicants get prompted for additional documents after submission, each with a clear reason — so the request doesn’t feel like a dead end.

2 / 3

Verification failed

Rejections show specific, fixable reasons so users can debug and resubmit correctly — instead of guessing and abandoning.

3 / 3

Continue later

Institutional clients need time to gather documents, so progress is saved and the flow is resumable from where they left off.

06 · Final design

The flow

Select entity type → document checklist → guided submission.

  • Select entity type — jurisdiction-specific routing.
  • Document checklist — exactly what to prepare before starting.
  • Guided submission — company info, contact person, required documents, and an additional-document step — with the right-document decision made at each fork.

Full journey — entry, preparation, and the four submission steps.

Key screens — the guided form steps where comprehension decisions happen.

08 · Hindsight

Self-awareness

What launch taught me — and would change next.

After launch, first-time pass rate rose only marginally (41% → 42%) — well short of the projected lift. The more useful question was why it wasn’t larger, so I sat down with customer support and the compliance team.

  • The real gap: hidden requirements. The interface never showed the detailed document rules — so users failed checks they were never told about. A business certificate needed a certified-true-copy stamp, not just the original. Some documents needed a manual signature that users almost always skipped.
  • What I’d change: make every rule visible at the point of upload. Guiding users to the right document isn’t enough — I’d also show what makes it valid (stamp required, signature needed) right where they upload, not buried elsewhere.
  • Ship the highest-impact piece first. I’d scope the rollout with engineering earlier, so guided routing lands first and the metric can move sooner — instead of a partial release.
