Overview

One investigation crossed four tools that were never designed to connect

OKLink's compliance suite is two products: On-chain AML for alerts and address and entity profiles, and Chaintelligence, a separate site for mapping fund flows. One investigation crosses four of their surfaces, but nobody had designed the path between them, so analysts finished cases in XMind and spreadsheets and usage stayed low. New regulation in Hong Kong made the suite a priority.

01 · How analysts actually investigate

Observing 3 compliance officers work real cases set three design principles

While the team debated what to build, I proposed watching first. I interviewed 3 compliance officers across APAC and the US and sat in on real cases, mapping each step, tool and breakdown.

Who
Compliance investigators

AML investigators working the alert queue, and compliance officers at exchanges, OTC desks and institutions. Often on a small laptop, for a full shift.

Trigger
A flagged transaction

A transfer crosses a risk rule, often around US$2,000. The analyst asks who else is involved, how much moved and whether it repeats.

Goal
A pattern, documented

Document the suspicious pattern, then close the case, escalate it, or export the fund-flow map as evidence for management or the regulator.

Findings & Principles
15–45 minutes per alert

Low and medium alerts get about 15 minutes; high and severe ones 30 to 45. Anything an analyst has to hunt for comes out of that.

1Follow the analyst's reading order
Every decision gets reviewed

Decisions go to reviewers in other regions and, in the end, to regulators. A score nobody can explain doesn't survive that.

2Every score shows its source
The real analysis happens in XMind

The real synthesis happened outside the product: colour groups and notes in XMind, comparisons in spreadsheets.

3Bring the outside work inside
And one surprise

"It lacks features like A, B and C." Shown where they were: "Oh, I didn't know those features are there. They're what we need."

So each stage made existing features findable before adding new ones.
More from the sessionsJourney map, observations, graph gaps, constraints
What observation surfaced that a feature request would not
  • A small risk percentage is not automatically safe. Analysts check how often it repeats: a known laundering pattern is many small deposits and one large withdrawal, and each deposit on its own looks minor.
  • Some accounts carry no identity data at all. Non-disclosure broker sub-accounts have no KYC attached, so the first question on those alerts is whether this is a sub-account or the master. The old page made analysts answer it by copying an ID into a second tab; one account in the sample had 129 sub-accounts.
Two gaps in the old graph
1Analysts think in cases; the tool thought in addresses

Three entry points (address graph, transaction map, transaction path) forced a technical choice before the analyst had any context.

2The layout hid direction

Hundreds of nodes around a centre showed what was connected, not which way the money moved.

Two constraints beyond the graph
Constraint
Labels stay internal

A label can name a person, and the bodies that receive a report don't share intelligence. Labels show to the analyst and stay off anything shared outward.

Request
Bulk tracing

"Ten addresses, then their common counterparties one layer out, two layers, up to twenty. Right now I do it in a spreadsheet." This became multi-hop tracing in section 03.

02 · Stage 1 · Triage

Alert page: four answers in the order analysts ask for them

Alert page · On-chain AML / KYT Handed over 1Reading order

Every analyst asked the same four questions in the same order, so I laid the page out by those questions instead of by data source:

  1. 1What share is risky
  2. 2How much moved
  3. 3Where it came from
  4. 4Which way it went
  • Three bandsWhy the alert fired, the evidence behind it, and the actions available.
  • One step to the next stageThe user record and the fund-flow graph open from the alert itself.
  • Write-up in placeAssignment, status, findings and conclusion sit next to what they refer to.
03 · Stage 2 · Assess

Address risk: every score traces back to the original sanctions listing

Address risk · On-chain AML / KYA Shipped 2Visible source

The FATF-aligned score breaks down in layers: five risk dimensions built from 14+ configurable risk factors, then three evidence tables that go down to the original sanctions listing. I made every layer open to its source, because a score nobody can explain doesn't survive review. An analyst can answer "how do you know?" without leaving the page.

Detected risks: five dimensions on a radar, the categories that fired beside it, and three tabs into the evidence.

Each blocklist row names the category, the listing body and a link to the original, so the analyst can open the OFAC record itself.

Rules for dense evidence, and multi-hop tracingMulti-hop handed over
  • Pagination only above five rows. Up to twenty rows scroll; beyond that the list stops rather than implying it's complete.
  • The category chart caps at eight slices. Long amounts switch to abbreviated notation, so a dense table stays readable.
  • Stablecoins and native coins resolve differently, so the transaction table treats each as its own case.
  • Unbuilt depth stays visible. It shows as a disabled control with a "not currently supported" note, so analysts learn where the tool stops instead of guessing.
Multi-hop tracing, from the bulk-tracing request

Trace a target address outward layer by layer, up to 50 addresses, with first and last transaction times on hover. I designed and specified it from the same research; it was with engineering when I left.

Multi-hop analysis: the risk-path list, one asset's transaction map, and the detail panel for a related address.

04 · Stage 3 · Identify

Entity profiles: a new module that shows who is behind an address

Entity profiles · On-chain AML / KYE Shipped · 0→1 search 2Visible source

An address tells you what moved, not who you're dealing with. KYE answers that for exchanges, mixers and services. I designed how analysts get to an entity: the landing page, search and the category list. Another designer did the entity page.

Three layouts tried by colleagues; all 3 picked the split view

The design team disagreed in internal review about how to structure the entry. Rather than keep arguing, I asked 3 colleagues to try three layouts on the same tasks, such as "find the darknet-market entity in the United Kingdom".

  1. Option 1.1 · One long page

    Search, categories and the full entity table stacked on one scroll.

  2. Option 1.2 · Split viewsPicked

    Search first; categories and entities on separate tabs.

  3. Option 2 · List first

    No landing page; open straight on the entity table with search and a category filter.

Why 1.2

All 3 found 1.2 the easiest to understand: search comes first, and categories don't blur into entities.

Their comments also shaped the final design: "KYE" alone wasn't clear to them, but the full name was, so the navigation says "Know Your Entity".
Four releases, and what each field may claimRelease history and two data questions
  1. MVPSearch, list and entity detail
  2. V1.1.0Detail, list and watchlist
  3. V1.1.1Design QA and a regression pass

Many open questions weren't about layout but about what the data could honestly support, and each was settled with product and the data team before the field shipped.

Fiat accepted: a count or a list?

A count fits the table; a list is what a compliance officer needs. The answer changed the column in the entity list.

What should search match?

Exact, fuzzy or alias matching decides whether an analyst finds an entity under its local trading name or misses it.

05 · Stage 4 · Trace and report

Fund-flow graph: laid out the way money moves, and written up without leaving it

Fund-flow graph · Chaintelligence Shipped · 1-month redesign 1Reading order 3Work inside

The old graph was a star that hid which way money moved, and the write-up happened in XMind after an export. The redesign follows the analyst from opening a graph to writing it up. The biggest change was the layout: I chose a left-to-right flow because analysts read a case as money moving from source to destination. The other five steps are folded below.

1
Layout: from a star to a flow
User pain point

The radial layout became a left-to-right flow that reads the way money moves.

Tools follow the selection

The scattered tool rail became three groups: save and export, add and filter, edit. Edit actions appear only for what's selected.

With several addresses selected

The graph offers "Show mutual counterparts", the comparison analysts had been doing by hand in a spreadsheet.

Trade-off: ship in phases, not all at once

After discussing it with the PM, I split the graph redesign into phases and implemented it feature by feature, so each feature could ship when it was ready instead of waiting for the whole redesign.

The cost was that some phases went out incomplete. When one couldn't supply risk-profile data yet, we dropped that column rather than ship it blank: a column that's always empty teaches people to skim past the table, and on a compliance screen every visible field has to be trustworthy.
Five more steps: list, create, expand, add, synthesiseThe full six-step redesign
2
Graph list: one list of cases
Design insight

Analysts build a graph around a case, not a single address. Three entry types became one list of case graphs, and light mode became the default for analysis.

3
Create graph: start in the middle
Design insight

The entry moved from a bar at the top to the centre of the empty canvas, styled like the address node it creates, and it takes several addresses at once.

4
Source added: counterparties by default
User pain point

The old graph dropped one node onto an empty canvas. Now it expands one layer of senders and receivers automatically, so the analyst starts with the immediate picture.

5
Add more addresses from where you are
Design insight

Investigators gather suspicious addresses offline. They can now add them from a counterparty's related-addresses panel, from empty canvas, or from the transaction panel, not only from the top bar.

6
Synthesise: bring the XMind step inside
Design insight

Analysts grouped evidence by colour and named the groups in XMind. Both moved into the tool, a colour palette for nodes and a summary label for a group, so the exported graph is already the report.

07 · Outcome

Three stages shipped, two handed over as specs

After launch, an analyst can go from an alert to a written-up graph inside the product, and every risk score opens to its source. The alert page and multi-hop tracing went to engineering as finished specs.

Shipped

  • Address risk analysis with traceable scores
  • Entity search, landing page and category list: MVP and three follow-up releases

How I'd measure it
Reading order
Alert review time

Time from opening an alert to a decision, against the 15–45 minute budget, watching high and severe alerts most closely.

Work inside
Exports to XMind

How many cases still get exported and rebuilt in XMind. If in-graph synthesis works, this should fall.

Work inside
Uses of "Show mutual counterparts"

Uses per case, as a measure of how much spreadsheet comparison it replaced.

I specified event tracking for the graph redesign, but it didn't ship before I left and no baseline was captured, so these are the measures I'd start with, not results.

What worked, and what I'd change
1
Watching the work found problems no request mentioned

The 15–45 minute budget, the detour into XMind and the copy-paste into a second tab all came from sitting in on real cases. Ordering four tools as one investigation mattered more than polishing any single screen.

2
Next time: measurement ships in the first release

The tracking was specified but didn't ship before I left, and there was no baseline, so I can describe what changed but not how much time it saved. Next time I'd agree a baseline with product during design and make tracking part of the first release.
