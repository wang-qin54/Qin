Why it mattered

In 2024 Australia and the EU both introduced stricter crypto KYC rules. Without compliant onboarding, the exchange risked losing access to two of its biggest markets, and the requirements kept changing while compliance negotiated with the regulators.

02 · Problem

One short flow had to become two regulated journeys

Australian and European users used to share one five-step KYC flow. New rules in both regions required stricter Level 2 checks before anyone could reach derivatives, and each regulator asked for different things.

One five-step flow became two regulated journeys: Australia splits retail from wholesale, and the EU adds disclosures and declarations.

🇦🇺 Australia · AUSTRAC + ASIC

Two user types, one fixed test

  • Type sets access. Each user is classified as retail or wholesale, and that decides what they can use.
  • Wording can't change. Retail users must pass ASIC's suitability test before using derivatives, and its questions can't be reworded or coached.
🇪🇺 Europe · MiCA

More screening, warnings users must read

  • Screening and classification. PEP and sanctions screening, investor risk classification, and source of funds above set thresholds.
  • No buried warnings. Risk warnings before key actions belong in the flow, not in the legal small print.
The design question
How do you make a strict, pass/fail compliance flow feel fair, without coaching users toward the right answers? Requirements also kept shifting during the build, so the design had to absorb change without losing its structure.
03 · Solution

Two flows on one shared core

Both markets share the same identity core (photo ID, face scan, personal details), then branch into their own Level 2 rules. Each screen asks for one thing and says what it unlocks.

Australia · AUSTRAC + ASIC

One choice, two branches: after basic verification, retail users take the suitability test (branch 1) and wholesale users upload evidence (branch 2).

Europe · MiCA

Disclosure before access: after identity and documents come the appropriateness test, disclosures and declarations, plus proof of assets above a threshold.

04 · Key decisions

Three decisions that made strict rules usable

The quiz went through four rounds before it worked for both compliance and users. First, I benchmarked how five exchanges handle tiered verification and knowledge tests. Two patterns held across all five: one question per page, and education before the test. Both shaped the first two decisions.

Coinbase
Guide before the quiz: educate, then assess.
Binance
Levels side by side: each benefit is explicit.
Kraken
One matrix: time to complete vs. limits unlocked.
OKX
Requirements first: limits listed before one Verify button.
Bitget
Benefits up front: what each level earns.

01One question per page

Problem
A long scrolling quiz of fixed legal wording was quick to build but hard to read.
Decision
One question per screen, with progress by topic so users see the full scope up front.
Trade-off
More taps, in exchange for comprehension where it legally matters.

02Educate before the gate

Problem
Novice users met a hard pass/fail quiz cold, so they either guessed or gave up.
Decision
An optional crypto guide before the quiz. After a missed attempt, users see which topics to review and a way back to the guide.
Trade-off
No hints inside the quiz itself. The guide sits outside it, as education only.

03Let users choose their path

Problem
In Australia, retail and wholesale users had different requirements and benefits, and there was no existing pattern to follow.
Decision
A user-type choice at the start of Level 2 that shows what each path asks for and unlocks. Then it branches: retail to the suitability test, wholesale to evidence upload.
Trade-off
One extra choice up front, so no one sees steps that don't apply to them.
05 · Changing rules

Three months of changing rules

Over three months of build, requirements changed on short notice. I worked directly with compliance and legal and reshaped the flow each month: a wireframe to align understanding, then revised benefits, then the branched flow.

Built once, composed per market

To keep two markets consistent, each screen type was designed once (forms, questionnaires, declarations, document upload) and composed differently per jurisdiction.

After launch: a modular KYC framework

Once both markets shipped, I worked with the product and design team to turn these patterns into a framework for future markets: a global identity core, plus a regional branch per jurisdiction.

06 · Migration

Re-verify without starting over

Existing users moving to the EU entity had to re-verify under MiCA. Instead of starting over with a blank account, the flow reads where each user already is and asks only for what MiCA still requires.

07 · Outcome

Both markets approved, one framework to reuse

  • MiCA licence granted

    The EU flow was demoed to the regulator as part of KuCoin's MiCA licence application.

  • AUSTRAC registration confirmed

    The Australian entity is registered with AUSTRAC.

  • The crypto guide shipped

    Compliance accepted it as education, not coaching, so it shipped as a product feature.

  • A template for new markets

    The modular framework became the team's starting point for future jurisdictions.

On metrics: these were compliance-gated MVPs, so success meant clearing the regulator. I don't have post-launch funnel data; conversion would have been the next phase.

What I learned
  1. 01

    Compliance is a design material

    The best decisions, like the pre-quiz guide, came from designing with the compliance officer in the room, not around them.

  2. 02

    Go modular from day one

    I distilled the framework after launch; starting with it would have saved rework across the two markets.

  3. 03

    Scope migration early

    Migration came in late; designing it alongside the main flows would have been smoother.
