System overview

Six modules, one Wealthsight tool

Wealthsight is the tool banks use to run model-portfolio investing. Portfolio managers create model portfolios from an instrument universe, rebalance client accounts to them, and track the orders and trades that follow. Day to day I designed across the whole tool — wireframes, high-fidelity screens and client demos.

02 · Deep dive · Account dashboard

One account dashboard for three products

Problem
The same client-account page had to serve portfolio managers, advisors and individual investors — three products, three designers — with little prior research and a client deadline.
Method
I planned and ran a workshop with the other two designers and we gathered desktop research together. I did the analysis and summary, then checked the concept in a guerrilla walkthrough with 5 colleagues.

What made a single pattern hard

Multi-faceted requirements — business, clients & maintenance

Incorporate multi-faceted requirements from clients, business value, user experience and technical strategy perspectives. Define a shared design pattern that fit into three different journeys: portfolio management tool, financial advisor and customers.

One pattern for customers & professionals

It is challenging for three designers to agree on one design pattern. Because enterprise users and customers can have totally different goals and focus, this could lead to opposite designs. E.g. to show or hide certain features depends on the frequency of use.

Limited prior research

Because of the timeline to ship the feature to clients — and because some were concepts for a marketing pitch with no existing users asking for them — we had limited knowledge and resources to understand users’ needs and mental models.

03 · Deep dive · Model portfolio module

Creating a model portfolio, then reading it

Portfolio managers enter model portfolios here; bank staff then recommend each model to clients whose risk score matches it. I redesigned both ends of the module: the flow that creates a portfolio, and the table that shows how it performs.

Part 1 · Making creation hard to get wrong

Problem
Creating a model portfolio meant many linked compliance fields, with errors shown only at the end. Even internal staff got confused, and the flow was hard to demo to prospective clients.
Method
With no access to users, a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics, a platform audit, and interviews with the product owner and engineers.
Decision
Split creation into 4 steps with a stepper, mark required fields, and validate linked fields step by step. Regroup the view page so managers find what they came for.
Result
After launch, clients raised no complaints about the flow, and the company onboarded a new client — not credited to this redesign alone.

Sole designer · 1 PM, 1 front-end engineer · 2 months

What the heuristic evaluation found

Prevent errors up front

Many fields are interlinked, and learning that from an error popup at the end is frustrating. Make the prerequisites visible before people need them.

Efficient navigation, less distraction

Portfolio managers arrive with a clear target. Let them get to the information they want quickly.

A flexible journey, not a fixed line

People come to this page for different reasons, so they need more than one way to move through it.

Part 2 · Testing 4 layouts for the metrics table

Problem
On the detail page, managers were getting the option to add instruments and benchmarks to the performance view, so one table had to compare several portfolios across returns, volatility, Sharpe and Sortino over seven time periods.
Method
Research access was limited, so I ran a comparative usability test with 5 colleagues. Three variables — colour coding, which axis holds the portfolios, tabs or a dropdown — became 4 layouts. Testers rated each for ease of comparison (1–5), some in reversed order, then explained their scores in follow-up interviews.
Decision
Option 2: colour-coded rows, portfolios down the side and time periods across the top, with tabs to switch metric.
Result
Option 2 scored highest (32, against 25, 23 and 18). 3 of 5 interviewees said it worked like the spreadsheets they already use.

04 · Other modules

Model portfolios, instruments, rebalance, orders and trades

The rest of the tool covers what happens after a portfolio manager decides to act. These screens are built from the same design system, so dense tables, filters and status patterns behave the same everywhere.

Model portfolios

Portfolio managers maintain a library of model portfolios that client accounts follow.

  • Each card surfaces the facts needed to pick a model: risk level (PRR), currency, instrument type, benchmark and publish status.
  • Latest publish and rebalance dates sit side by side, so stale models are easy to spot.

Instruments

The instrument universe that model portfolios are built from.

  • Each row pairs the instrument name with its ISIN, asset class, category and benchmark.
  • Asset class can be edited in place, with the original classification kept underneath for reference.
  • Ten filters — watchlist, category, benchmark, type, currency, asset class, complexity, risk, segment and channel — narrow a long list quickly.

Rebalance: review the buy/sell list before submitting

A rebalance can touch many model portfolios at once. The buy/sell list turns that batch into something a portfolio manager can check before any order is created.

  • Left column lists every model portfolio affected by the current filter (here 12 of 20), so the scope of the batch is visible up front.
  • For each instrument the table shows weight before, after and the difference, with the resulting Buy, Sell or N/A action.
  • Sells and buys are colour-coded and cash is shown as its own line, so the net effect is readable at a glance.

Orders: track status and catch exceptions

Every buy, sell and rebalance instruction lands in the orders table, where operations and portfolio managers follow it to completion.

  • Order status (Pending, Complete, Error, Disable) sits next to the action, so failed orders stand out in a long list.
  • Each row links the order to its account, trading account, model portfolio, order ID and creation time.
  • Filters for action, status, model portfolio, date, creator and account, plus expandable rows and full export for reconciliation.

Trades: notional and reconciliation

The trades view records the outcome of each order instruction, so teams can reconcile it.

  • Rows carry action, account code, trading account, group code, model portfolio, notional and order instruction ID.
  • Reconciliation status and portfolio segment filters help teams find trades that still need checking.
05 · Foundation

One design system under three products

Problem
Colours missed WCAG contrast, type sizes had drifted, and design and front-end used different names for the same things.
Method
Audited existing patterns across the product, ran feedback sessions with designers and the web team, then structured workshops on how to use the system.
Decision
Start from Material Design, use it flexibly, and keep one pattern library across all three products. A simple rule for every new screen: reuse a component, adjust it, or create and document a new one.
Result
New modules were assembled from existing components. Updated components were shared through a monthly design × web meeting and a shared channel.

Led the rebuild · 3 designers, 5 front-end engineers

Style guide: contrast and type

I updated colours to meet WCAG contrast, refined font sizes from designer and web-team feedback, and aligned naming with the front-end so a style in Figma had the same name in code.

Guidelines: when to confirm, when to undo

Components came with rules for when to use them. A confirmation box is only worth the interruption when an action is irreversible, severe, complex and rare; everything else gets an undo toast.

More from the design system: components, writing guide, grid, and how we ran it

New components

Modals, chips, breadcrumbs, scrollspy and snackbar, added where products kept building their own versions.

06 · What it led to

Across these projects I backed design decisions with whatever evidence I could get: a guerrilla walkthrough for the dashboard, a heuristic evaluation for creation, a comparative usability test for the metrics table. When I presented the dashboard and its walkthrough results, the founder asked me to plan the research for a new product — what advisors need, what to build, and in what order.

Research
With the product manager, I interviewed 5 financial advisory directors, mostly from Swiss investment banks, recruited through the founder’s network.
Synthesis
