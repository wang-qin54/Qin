05 · The turn

The review kept the structure and took the layering

Leadership’s sign-off was on the hierarchy, and it was specific: crypto and securities finally read as one aligned layout, the account switch had a visible relationship to the content it drove, and — the line I cared about most — holdings and coin balances had stopped being two separate things. The design lead’s note landed at a different layer. It was not a rejection of the direction; it reshaped how the direction looked. Three things went, and each one bought something.

  • 1Dark layering behind the hero — separated the account world from the page. Gone after review: one visual language, and a dark mode that costs a fraction to build.
  • 2Buttons in their own accent colours — folded into the global palette, which was still being decided.
  • 3Card layer beneath the account tabs — bound the switch to the content it drives. Flattened — the one I would still argue for.

Crypto tab, before and after the review. The information architecture is identical in both — every change is at the surface. Design boards captured in the product’s original zh-CN; there was never an English version of these two.

What the review removed What it bought
RemovedThe dark layering behind the hero BoughtOne visual language instead of two. A dark mode built from a flat unified surface costs a fraction of one built from a layered surface.
RemovedThe card layer beneath the account tabs BoughtA calmer, more uniform page — paid for with a weaker visual link between the account switch and the content it drives.
RemovedButtons in their own accent colours BoughtButtons inherit the global palette, which was still being decided at the time. Nothing on this page would have to be re-decided later.

Securities tab — the same trade applied consistently. Structure intact, layering gone. Design boards captured in zh-CN.

The trade, stated plainly I traded visual hierarchy for consistency and an affordable dark mode. Of the three removals I’d still argue for the second — it is the one carrying meaning rather than style. But consistency across every screen in the product is not a call that belongs to one page’s designer. What I was accountable for — the information architecture — went through the review unchanged, which is why the audit in section 06 reads the way it does.
06 · Outcome

The structure was tested by changes I didn’t make

  • Leadership aligned on the information hierarchy and approved the redesign to move into implementation — after almost two years stalled.
  • Then the product kept growing underneath it. Over the following months the same page absorbed a second fiat currency, a full risk-control system, yield promoted to a headline feature, Hong Kong stocks alongside US, and credit that moves both ways between crypto and securities. Separately, our design leader ran a company-wide design-system unification.
  • I had no hand in the pivot or the migration — which is what makes the comparison a fair test of the structure.

Three months on, I put the two builds side by side and audited them layer by layer. The visual language had been replaced and the scope had grown considerably. The load-bearing structure was untouched.

  • 1Total assets with its trend — one headline number, one line.
  • 2The account and its P&L — the switcher drives every figure below it.
  • 3Recent records — three rows, then the list.
  • 4The asset-class switch — same control; the order flipped to put securities first.
  • 5Net assets and the accounts that add up to it — the distribution expands under the number in both.

The same tab, three months apart. Read them top-down and the order is identical, line for line: total assets with its trend, the account and its P&L, the action row, recent records, the asset-class switch, net assets, and the accounts that add up to it. What changed is surface and scope — an entirely replaced visual language, the asset-class order flipped to put securities first, and a two-way credit system (a credit in figure and its transfer entry) that did not exist at alignment. Web came second

This case is the app on purpose. The people we designed for — high-net-worth individuals running several accounts at once — live in the app and use the web far less, so the app carried the problem definition, the exploration and the review. The web product followed, and inherited the hierarchy rather than re-deriving it. Before that, web had four separate pages that did not talk to each other: no combined number, no shared risk read, a different account switcher on each.

The shipped legacy web product before alignment, captured in zh-CN; browser chrome and the empty right-hand column cropped away. Web has since been rebuilt on the app’s structure; it is not the subject of this case.

Why this is the evidence I trust Anyone can defend a layout on the day they present it. Surviving a product pivot and a design-system migration untouched is the part you can’t argue your way into — and it means those calls were structural, not aesthetic.
  • What did change is the interesting part. Taking one topic deep removed the scroll rhythm people had been orienting by — a navigation debt nobody files as a bug. I closed it with rules rather than fixes: one element keeps its size in the sticky header, a spacing scale so section rhythm stops depending on who drew the screen that week.
  • Still no post-launch metrics. The proof here is structural durability and adoption, which is real, but it is not a number. I’d rather say that plainly than dress it up.
07 · Reflection

What I’d do differently

This was not a research-led, metrics-rich case, and it is worth being plain about the shape of it. What I owned, and what I didn’t:

What I owned

  • ✓ Information architecture
  • ✓ Prioritization & reading path
  • ✓ Interaction design
  • ✓ Clickable HTML prototypes & daily stakeholder loop

What I didn’t own

  • ✗ Product strategy
  • ✗ Timeline / release window
  • ✗ Engineering resources
  • ✗ Research budget
Looking back I’d instrument it. With no research budget and one week to align a two-year-stalled screen, the success criterion was decision quality — a hierarchy leadership could approve. Everything I can prove is structural: the skeleton survived, the specs were adopted. What I can’t show is a number. The outcome I value most isn’t the UI anyway — it’s creating clarity in a project that had been blocked for two years, and building it so the clarity outlasted me. Next time: sell the value of the direction earlier, and scope a phased MVP so the stronger ideas from the exploration reach users sooner.
