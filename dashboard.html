Context and role

A dense institutional tool, designed with almost no user access

Real users were out of reach, so I backed each decision with the cheapest evidence I could get, and let the results make the case for proper research.

  • Owned Wealthsight’s B2B portfolio management tool: the account dashboard from scratch, and the other five modules redesigned, from wireframes to client demos.
  • Shared dashboard: facilitated the work with the other two designers; I did the analysis and the final solution.
  • Design system: led the rebuild the three products share.
  • New product: planned the research the CEO asked for, and ran the interviews.

No users, no research habit, fixed demo dates

  • No access to bank usersPortfolio managers sat inside client banks, and recruiting them was not possible.
  • Research was new hereFew stakeholders had seen user research, so there was no budget or process for it.
  • Client deadlinesDesigns had to be ready for sales demos and client releases.

What each audience got, and gave up

Managers wanted depth, advisors wanted something to show a client, and investors wanted it simple. With one shared front-end library, all three had to share one layout.

  • Portfolio manager

    WantedDepth across several dimensions.

    GotFilters, sortable headers and extra columns on the holdings list.

    Gave upCharts open on load. They’re collapsed to protect load time.

  • Financial advisor

    WantedThe allocation, to talk a client through it.

    GotAllocation and performance in the first fold.

    Gave upProposals as a dashboard module. They now sit one tab away.

  • Individual investor

    WantedSimple over complete.

    GotA short default list and a calmer view with charts collapsed.

    Gave upTransactions on the page. They’re in a tab, hidden by default.

My callOne layout with different defaults. Professionals add depth; the retail default stays short.

What everyone paysOne extra step to open charts, proposals or transactions.

Four problems the heuristic review found

I reviewed the flow against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics and audited the platform, then interviewed the product owner and engineers to check the field logic heuristics can’t.

  • No required-field cue until submitVisibility of system status · error prevention · recognition over recall
  • Linked fields with no cue, only an error afterwardsError prevention
  • Locked fields that looked editable after creationRecognition over recall
  • A detail page that needed a long scrollAesthetic and minimalist design

Step 4 of 5 · Design system

A design system written as rules, not just components

Colours missed WCAG contrast, type sizes had drifted, and design and front-end used different names for the same things. I led the rebuild of the library all three products shared, on a Material Design base; most of the work was agreeing with developers what to build first and what to call it.

Led the rebuild · 3 designers, 5 front-end engineers

Rule 1

Confirm only the irreversible; undo the rest

A confirmation box only when an action is irreversible, severe, complex and rare; everything else gets an undo toast.

Rule 2

One question per dialog

When a dialog is needed, its copy follows rules too, so dialogs read the same across all three products.

Where the rules came from: a dialog audit

Grouping every dialog by job showed developers how often the same pattern was being rebuilt, so dialogs became the first priority we agreed together.

Pain points

  • Massive information. Suppose everything in one application — still need to switch screens a lot, so many screens and note-taking are involved.
  • Various different channels and screens to refer (internal, market, performance, preference, etc).
  • Manual calculation. Do a lot of calculation to correct wrong data of bonds.

Results and reflection

What the evidence earned

None of the evidence above was perfect, but each piece moved a decision, and together they changed how the CEO saw research.

  1. Dashboard

    A demo that helped win a client

    Sales showed prospective clients the new dashboard and its new capabilities, and HSBC Hong Kong came on board.

  2. Creation and metrics table

    Signed off by the CEO

    The CEO and product owner passed the creation flow in review, and the CEO backed the metrics layout after taking the test himself.

  3. New product

    Access to real users

    The CEO asked me to plan research for a new advisor product, and opened his own network to recruit for it.

Reflection: the cheapest evidence first

What worked. At the start I felt stuck: asking for research went nowhere. Lean and guerrilla methods changed my question from “how do I get users?” to “what is the cheapest evidence for this decision?” Showing results, not asking for budget, is what got me real users.

The limit. None of these tests reached a portfolio manager. They settled whether screens were easy to use, not whether they fit a manager’s job, and nothing shipped with usage data to check.

What I’d measure with real access

  • Creation flow: how long it takes to create a model portfolio, and how long it takes to find key information on one that already exists.
  • Design system: how often each component is reused after an update, the time a consistent pattern saves per screen, and a short 1–5 rating from designers and developers on how well it supports their work, followed up with interviews.
